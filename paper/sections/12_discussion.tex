\section{Discussion: what the model establishes}
The integrated result is best interpreted by separating three questions. First, INL establishes the chemical-process requirement: 176.8 MWth at the final high-temperature operating point. Second, Nishihara et al. establishes that the selected 600 MWth source architecture contains a 370 MWth heat branch, so the required process duty is within source heat capacity. Third, the remaining 423.2 MWth is only thermal capacity; without a validated off-design turbine and internal-load model it is not converted into a project electricity-export claim.

This distinction also strengthens the economics. The controlling S\$0/MWh case does not require electricity revenue. Instead, it charges the full selected source-product economic burden while giving no value to uncertain project power output. The doubled IHX/secondary-loop case remains only an adverse source cost sensitivity, not a capacity argument.

The lifecycle abatement result remains dominated by the same-output direct-emissions difference and lower natural-gas use. Cross-border CCS availability, mature-design reactor economics, the direct-heat lifecycle proxy and detailed reactor/process integration remain important screening limitations.


\subsection{From screening result to engineering interpretation}
The deeper pass does not change the canonical 85\% result; it changes what can be inferred from it.  First, reactor selection is now process-driven: a roughly 900$^\circ$C heating medium is required, so water-cooled SMRs are alternative electricity/augmented-heat architectures rather than direct substitutes for the selected high-temperature helium route.  HTTR demonstrates the temperature regime but not project scale, while GTHTR300C supplies the closest published process-heat architecture.

Second, nominal source heat capacity is not component qualification.  The 176.8 MWth process duty is within the approximately 370 MWth high-hydrogen source branch but about 4\% above one published 170 MWth reference IHX.  Creep, fatigue, inspection, permeation and lifetime therefore remain genuine component gates.

Third, nuclear/chemical isolation is finite rather than absolute.  Primary helium does not flow directly into the chemical plant, but measured HTTR tritium in secondary helium shows that radionuclide permeation must be assessed.  Likewise, Japanese process-trip experiments support thermal buffering/control but do not replace a project-scale transient model.

Fourth, emergency planning is a consequence-and-policy result rather than a generic advanced-reactor radius.  Chinese HTR-PM research supports much smaller technical EPZs than conventional large-reactor practice, yet the implemented Chinese HTR-PM emergency plan retained kilometre-scale plume zones.  Singapore therefore requires its own sequence/frequency/source-term/dispersion/dose/protective-action analysis.

Finally, the sensitivity screens reveal which assignment thresholds are actually fragile.  The no-backup annual-abatement screen crosses 0.25 MtCO$_2$e/y at about 23.2\% effective availability.  Partial CCS is less critical to the assignment threshold than expected because nuclear heat alone displaces substantial natural-gas combustion, although CCS remains important to the proposed low-direct-emissions architecture.  These are screening robustness statements, not plant-reliability or capture-operability predictions.
