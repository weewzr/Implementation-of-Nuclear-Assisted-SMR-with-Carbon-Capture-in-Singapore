\section{Introduction and research question}


The physical idea can be stated before any reactor terminology: methane supplies carbon and hydrogen to the chemical process, while a furnace normally burns additional fuel to supply high-temperature heat. Carbon capture addresses much of the carbon dioxide leaving the process; nuclear heat aims to reduce the separate combustion requirement. The research question is whether that extra nuclear system earns its complexity.

This research evaluates a proposed industrial hydrogen decarbonisation pathway under explicit abatement and cost screening criteria. Numerical thresholds are useful decision filters, but they are not sufficient on their own: a credible answer must also establish the physical mechanism, engineering feasibility and Singapore-specific conditions under which the concept could operate. This is a demanding setting for industrial decarbonisation. Natural gas supplied 94\% of Singapore's electricity-generation fuel mix in 2024~\cite{ema2024context}, while the country is also examining cross-border Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) because it does not have suitable domestic geological CO$_2$ storage resources~\cite{mti2025carbon}. Singapore is building capability to assess advanced nuclear technologies, but has not made a decision to deploy nuclear energy~\cite{mti2025nuclear,mti2026inir}. Nuclear power is therefore treated here as a future engineering scenario, not an existing Singapore asset.

The industrial process considered is conventional hydrogen production by Steam Methane Reforming (SMR). In simple terms, natural gas supplies methane, steam supplies water, and high-temperature heat drives reforming to a hydrogen-rich synthesis gas containing carbon monoxide. Water-Gas Shift (WGS) then reacts carbon monoxide with additional steam to form more hydrogen and CO$_2$, after which gas separation and Pressure Swing Adsorption (PSA) recover the hydrogen product~\cite{ieaghg2017smr}. This route has two conceptually different carbon sources. First, methane is the chemical feedstock, so part of its carbon becomes process CO$_2$. Second, the reformer itself needs substantial high-temperature heat, conventionally supplied by burning fuel, which produces additional combustion CO$_2$~\cite{ieaghg2017smr}.

CCS addresses an important part of that problem by separating a concentrated CO$_2$ stream and sending it toward conditioning, transport and permanent storage. It does not make natural-gas hydrogen carbon-free. The plant still consumes methane feedstock; the reformer still needs heat; upstream gas production and transport can emit greenhouse gases; capture and CO$_2$ conditioning require energy; and the captured stream needs a functioning transport-and-storage chain. For Singapore, that last step is necessarily linked to cross-border storage rather than a demonstrated domestic geological store~\cite{mti2025carbon,mti2025indonesiaccs}. The remaining engineering question is therefore whether the fossil-fired high-temperature heat service itself can be displaced while retaining the established reforming and capture pathway.

This motivates the nuclear-process-heat proposition tested in this paper. Instead of converting all reactor heat to electricity before serving an industrial load, a sufficiently high-temperature reactor can transfer heat through a heat exchanger to a separate process loop. The proposed heat path is
\[
\text{fission}\rightarrow\text{reactor heat}\rightarrow\text{primary helium}
\rightarrow\text{intermediate heat exchanger}\rightarrow\text{secondary helium}
\rightarrow\text{reformer heat}.
\]
The Intermediate Heat Exchanger (IHX) is important because the reactor primary coolant does not simply flow into the chemical reformer: heat crosses the exchanger boundary into a separate secondary-helium circuit. The detailed configuration and isolation logic are introduced in Section~\ref{sec:proposed-system}.

The temperature requirement makes the reactor class application-specific. The adopted Idaho National Laboratory (INL) process basis requires an 871$^\circ$C reformer outlet and 900$^\circ$C secondary-helium heat supply~\cite{inltev953,inltev961}. High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR) technology is therefore investigated because helium-cooled, graphite-moderated reactor systems can operate in this temperature class; Japan's High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR), for example, has demonstrated 950$^\circ$C reactor-outlet operation~\cite{jaeahttr}. This does not make an HTGR universally preferable to other reactors. It makes the HTGR family relevant to this particular direct high-temperature process-heat duty, whereas lower-temperature reactor concepts would require a different heat-upgrading or electrification architecture. Detailed reactor screening is left to the heat-integration analysis rather than assumed here.

The final proposed solution is nuclear-assisted SMR-H$_2$+CCS. Its preferred future configuration uses one 600 MWth GTHTR300C-class source to supply two 130 MMSCFD reforming/CCS trains at 353.6 MWth total useful process heat; conventional SMR+CCS is retained as the non-nuclear comparator, not as a replacement proposal. The research gap is consequently narrower than nuclear hydrogen or SMR with CCS, both of which already have substantial literature. What remains to be tested for this project is whether a literature-supported high-temperature nuclear heat source, an SMR-H$_2$ process with CCS, lifecycle emissions, represented economics and Singapore deployment constraints can be placed inside one consistent engineering boundary and still satisfy the specified screening criteria without treating screening success as proof of deployment feasibility. The originality of the study lies in that integrated Singapore-facing test and in making the evidence boundary explicit, not in claiming that nuclear process heat or carbon capture is new.

The primary research question is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Under Singapore-relevant industrial and infrastructure constraints, can an HTGR-class reactor supply the high-temperature process heat required by steam-methane-reforming hydrogen production with carbon capture at a scale that satisfies the specified emissions-abatement and cost criteria, and what technical, safety, infrastructure and deployment conditions limit that conclusion?}
\end{quote}

The paper answers this question in physical order. It first defines the conventional SMR-H$_2$ reference and the proposed HTGR-assisted SMR-H$_2$+CCS configuration, then fixes a common design basis and system boundary. Literature and design-study inputs establish the required process temperature, heat duty and compatible reactor/process-heat architecture. Engineering balances and reproducible Rust calculations then connect hydrogen production to direct and lifecycle carbon accounting and to the represented annual cost ledger. Bounded sensitivity screens test whether the numerical conclusion is fragile to selected uncertainties. Finally, the quantitative feasibility assessment is separated from the unresolved questions that govern real deployment, including component qualification, integrated nuclear/chemical safety, Singapore regulation and siting, and cross-border CO$_2$ storage. The model therefore combines source inputs, design bases, declared assumptions, engineering balances and derived calculations; it is not presented as a wholly first-principles plant simulation.
