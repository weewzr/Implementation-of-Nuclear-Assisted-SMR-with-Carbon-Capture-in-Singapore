# Research framing

## 1. Starting point

The student proposed an HTGR-assisted steam methane reforming process with amine carbon capture. The initial design removes:
1. the conventional HTGR steam turbine/generator electricity-conversion path; and
2. the natural-gas-fired reformer furnace.

The concept instead sends high-temperature helium through heat-transfer equipment to supply reformer and steam-generation duties.

This is the starting hypothesis.

## 2. What the starting concept gets right
- High-temperature nuclear reactors can provide process heat rather than only electricity.
- SMR contains high-temperature duties that make reactor temperature relevant.
- Reforming and shift chemistry creates CO2 streams that can be candidates for capture.
- Avoiding a power-conversion step may reduce conversion losses for heat-demanding duties.

## 3. What must not be assumed
- That all HTGR heat is useful process heat.
- That steam generation and reformer duty can be supplied by the same temperature stream.
- That direct helium coupling is safer or cheaper than an indirect intermediate loop.
- That removing electricity generation improves overall economics; electricity may be required elsewhere.
- That capture fraction equals lifecycle CO2 avoidance.
- That natural-gas hydrogen remains competitive with electrolysis once methane leakage and nuclear infrastructure are included.
- That Singapore will deploy nuclear power.

## 4. Primary research question

Under Singapore-relevant industrial and infrastructure constraints, can HTGR-assisted steam methane reforming with amine-based carbon capture achieve the CN4252 carbon-abatement and cost requirements while providing a technically defensible advantage over competing low-carbon hydrogen configurations under a consistent system boundary?

## 5. Secondary questions
1. What mass and energy flows define a defensible fired-SMR baseline?
2. Which SMR duties can be supplied by HTGR heat at realistic reactor outlet temperatures?
3. What fraction of reactor heat should be allocated to reforming, steam generation and CCS regeneration?
4. What are the effects of direct versus indirect heat coupling?
5. How do nuclear-electric and direct-nuclear-heat pathways compare?
6. How much CO2 remains after capture and downstream conditioning?
7. How sensitive are lifecycle emissions to methane leakage, capture rate and electricity assumptions?
8. What production scale is needed to exceed 0.25 MtCO2e/y abatement?
9. What assumptions are required to reach S$100/tCO2e?
10. Which Singapore-specific siting, regulatory and infrastructure constraints are decision-limiting?

## 6. Competing hypotheses

### H1 — Direct nuclear-heat benefit
An HTGR can supply a significant fraction of reformer and steam/utility heat at adequate temperature, lowering fuel use and lifecycle emissions enough to satisfy the project thresholds.

### H2 — CCS-dominant benefit
SMR + CCS without nuclear integration obtains most of the achievable emissions reduction, so nuclear heat adds relatively little marginal abatement.

### H3 — Electrification competitor
Nuclear-generated electricity used in an electrically heated reformer is technically or economically preferable to direct helium-to-reformer coupling.

### H4 — Methane boundary limitation
Upstream natural-gas emissions limit lifecycle performance enough that high capture rates cannot produce very low-carbon hydrogen.

### H5 — Non-methane competitor
Electrolysis or another low-carbon hydrogen pathway has lower lifecycle emissions and/or better long-run performance once a consistent system boundary is applied.

### H6 — Singapore feasibility limitation
The process can be thermodynamically feasible but fail overall feasibility because of nuclear siting/regulation, heat-network infrastructure, CCS logistics, cost, or scale.

## 7. Minimum comparison matrix

| Configuration | Fossil combustion for reformer | Nuclear heat | Nuclear electricity | CCS | Key uncertainty |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Fired SMR | Yes | No | No | No | Baseline intensity |
| Fired SMR + CCS | Yes | No | No | Yes | Capture + heat penalty |
| eSMR + CCS | Reduced/none | No | Optional | Yes | Electricity intensity |
| Nuclear-electric eSMR + CCS | No | Indirect | Yes | Yes | Power conversion |
| Direct HTGR-heated SMR + CCS | No/low | Yes | Optional | Yes | Heat interface |
| Electrolysis benchmark | No | Optional | Yes | Usually no | Electricity source |

## 8. Success criteria

A candidate configuration must be evaluated against:
- annual avoided CO2e [Mt/y]
- lifecycle hydrogen intensity [kg CO2e/kg H2]
- specific abatement cost [S$/tCO2e]
- thermal efficiency / useful heat fraction
- hydrogen production rate [kg H2/y]
- reactor heat demand [MWth]
- electricity demand [MWe]
- natural-gas demand
- capture rate
- residual and upstream emissions
- CO2 compression/transport/storage boundary
- uncertainty / sensitivity range

The CN4252 target is a gate condition, not an assumed result.

## 9. Scope boundary for the first model

Start with:
natural gas + steam + reactor heat/electricity
-> reforming
-> water-gas shift
-> gas treatment / H2 separation
-> CO2 capture
-> solvent regeneration
-> CO2 compression.

Then extend to:
- natural-gas upstream emissions
- nuclear fuel-cycle emissions
- CO2 transport
- geological storage
- infrastructure and economic constraints.

## 11. Initial literature/repository landscape

Relevant methodological precedents include HTTR/HTGR methane-steam-reforming coupling studies, IAEA HTGR process-heat studies, INL nuclear-integrated modelling tools, and public SMR process simulations.

No existing public repository found in the connected account directly implements this complete Singapore-specific problem.

## 12. Contradictory evidence that must remain visible

- Direct HTGR-hydrogen plant coupling remains a demonstration/integration challenge.
- eSMR can eliminate direct reformer-furnace combustion but introduces electricity demand and source assumptions.
- High capture fractions do not automatically imply low lifecycle hydrogen emissions.
- Singapore has not decided to deploy nuclear power.
- Singapore's CCS pathway is constrained by the need for regional transport/storage arrangements.

These are tests the project must resolve, not conclusions.
